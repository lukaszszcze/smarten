Regions of the USA

The USA is usually divided into five regions: the Northeast, the Southeast, the Midwest, the Southwest and the West. They are not official, so a border state can appear in different regions on different maps (Maryland is sometimes in the Southeast). The states of each region are in the next section.

RegionMain geographical features
Northeastthe Appalachian Mountains, the Atlantic Ocean, the Great Lakes; borders Canada to the north
Southeastthe Appalachian Mountains, the Atlantic Ocean, the Gulf of Mexico, the Mississippi River
Midwestthe Great Lakes, the Great Plains, the Mississippi River; borders Canada to the north
Southwestthe Rocky Mountains, the Colorado River, the Grand Canyon, the Gulf of Mexico; borders Mexico to the south
Westthe Rocky Mountains, the Sierra Nevada, the Mojave Desert, the Pacific Ocean; borders Canada to the north and Mexico to the south

Smaller regions people often name: New England (Maine, Vermont, New Hampshire, Massachusetts, Rhode Island, Connecticut), the Mid-Atlantic (Virginia, West Virginia, Pennsylvania, Maryland, Delaware, New Jersey), the Great Lakes (Minnesota, Wisconsin, Illinois, Indiana, Ohio, Michigan), the Central Plains (Iowa, Missouri, Kansas, Nebraska), the Pacific Northwest (Washington, Oregon, Idaho) and the Rocky Mountains (Utah, Colorado, New Mexico, Wyoming, Montana).

The lower 48 means the 48 states that touch each other, without Alaska and Hawaii. Sources use it for records such as "the tallest mountain in the lower 48".

Mountains, rivers, lakes and deserts

Mountains of the USA

The three major ranges are the Appalachians, the Rocky Mountains and the Sierra Nevada.

  • The Appalachian Mountains run for 1,500 miles along the east of the country, from northern Alabama to Maine. They are old, forested mountains (pine, spruce, birch, maple). Smaller ranges inside them: the Great Smoky Mountains, the Blue Ridge Mountains, the Green Mountains, the White Mountains and the Berkshires. Early colonists found them a barrier to moving west.
  • The Rocky Mountains (the Rockies) are the longest range in North America and the second longest in the world: 3,000 miles from New Mexico through Montana and far into Canada. The Continental Divide runs along them: water on one side flows to the Atlantic, on the other to the Pacific. They are fold mountains, formed where two tectonic plates meet. National parks there: Yellowstone, Rocky Mountain, Grand Teton, Glacier.
  • The Sierra Nevada runs about 400 miles through California (and a little of Nevada). It is home to the giant sequoias, Yosemite National Park and Lake Tahoe. These are fault-block mountains, formed along a fault in the Earth's crust.
  • The Cascade Range runs 700 miles along the northwest coast into Canada and is part of the volcanic Ring of Fire around the Pacific; the active volcano Mount Saint Helens is in it.
  • Also: the Alaska Range (the tallest range in the USA), the Brooks Range (over 700 miles across northern Alaska), the Adirondacks (New York; Adirondack Park is the largest state park in the USA) and the Ozarks (Missouri and Arkansas, the largest range between the Appalachians and the Rockies).

  • The Columbia (1,240 miles) is the largest river of the northwest: from the Canadian Rockies through Washington State to the Pacific. Its Grand Coulee Dam produces the most power of any dam in the USA.
  • The Hudson (315 miles, eastern New York) is short but important: the Erie Canal (1825) joined it to the Great Lakes, which helped New York City grow.

Lakes of the USA

  • The Great Lakes: Superior, Huron, Michigan, Erie and Ontario, on the border with Canada. Remember them as SMHEO: "Super Man Helps Every One". Only Lake Michigan is entirely in the USA. Together they are the largest group of freshwater lakes in the world (about 21% of the world's surface fresh water). Eight states have a Great Lakes shore; cities on them include Chicago, Cleveland, Buffalo, Milwaukee and Toronto.
  • Lake Superior is the largest and deepest of them (31,700 sq mi / 82,100 km², up to 1,335 ft (407 m) deep) and the largest freshwater lake in the world by area.
  • The Great Salt Lake (Utah) is the largest lake outside the Great Lakes. It is saltier than the ocean, so no fish live in it, only brine shrimp and algae.
  • Crater Lake (Oregon) is the deepest lake in the USA: 1,943 ft (592 m). It is a caldera lake, in the collapsed top of the volcano Mount Mazama.
  • Lake Tahoe, high in the Sierra Nevada on the California–Nevada border, is the second deepest.
  • Lake Okeechobee (south Florida) is large but only 9 feet deep on average; alligators, manatees, herons and bobcats live there.

  • Mount Logan (5,959 m / 19,551 ft), in Kluane National Park in the Yukon, on the border with Alaska, is Canada's highest mountain and the second highest in North America, after Denali.
  • Canada has more lakes than any other country: about three million, around 60% of the world's natural lakes. The Northwest Territories alone have more lakes than most countries. The largest lake entirely in Canada is Great Bear Lake.
  • The longest river system is the Mackenzie (4,241 km / 2,635 miles) in the Northwest Territories, the largest river flowing from North America into the Arctic.
  • At Lytton (British Columbia) the Thompson and Fraser rivers meet, and at first they look as if they do not mix (a confluence).
  • Niagara Falls, on the Canada–USA border, formed in the last Ice Age. The Horseshoe Falls on the Canadian side are the most powerful waterfall in North America, and the best views are from Canada.
  • The Bay of Fundy, between New Brunswick and Nova Scotia, has the highest tides in the world: the water rises and falls by up to 16 m, twice a day. At the Hopewell Rocks you can walk on the seabed at low tide and kayak there a few hours later.
  • Baffin Island (Nunavut) is Canada's largest island and the world's fifth largest, as big as Spain. Its Mount Thor has the world's highest vertical cliff (a drop of 1,250 m). Most of Canada's islands are in the Arctic Archipelago: 36,563 islands.

  • The Rockies have distinct seasons: warm, rainy summers and cold, snowy winters.
  • Not every desert is hot. The Great Basin Desert is often very cold, and most of its precipitation falls as snow.
  • Death Valley is the hottest and driest place in North America: a world record of 134°F (56.7°C) and less than two inches (5 cm) of rain a year.

Canada

  • Canada has a wide range of climates. The north is very cold, with long, snowy winters; most of northern Canada falls below −30°C (−22°F) in winter, and parts of the Arctic are frozen all year.
  • Snag, in the Yukon, recorded one of the coldest temperatures in North America: −63°C (−81°F) in February 1947.
  • In the south, summers can be warm or even hot. The coasts are milder than inland.
  • In the northern territories, and sometimes on the Prairies, you can see the Northern Lights (the aurora borealis).

Plants of the USA

  • Giant sequoias (Sierra Nevada) are the largest trees in the world: up to 270 ft tall, over 25 ft across and over 3,000 years old. The General Sherman Tree in Sequoia National Park is the world's largest tree by volume.
  • Coast redwoods (Redwood National Park, California) are the tallest living things on the planet.
  • The bristlecone pine (Great Basin Desert) is the oldest known living organism: some are over 5,000 years old.
  • Deserts: the Joshua tree (Mojave; its scientific name is Yucca brevifolia), the saguaro cactus (Sonoran), the creosote bush, sagebrush, yuccas, agaves and prickly pears.
  • Mountain forests: pine, spruce, birch and maple in the Appalachians; spruce, pine, oak, juniper and fir in the Rockies.

Animals of the USA

  • Yellowstone: bears and bison.
  • The Rockies: bighorn sheep, badgers, grizzly bears, black bears, coyotes, elk and white-tailed deer.
  • The Appalachians: squirrels, cottontail rabbits, white-tailed deer, wolves, beavers, black bears and the red-tailed hawk.
  • The deserts: lizards, snakes, bats, owls, jackrabbits, scorpions, pronghorns, the Mojave ground squirrel and the kangaroo rat.
  • Lake Okeechobee and the Everglades (Florida): alligators, manatees, herons, cranes, river otters and bobcats.

Elk is not always the same animal.

In North America an elk is a large deer, the wapiti. In British English "elk" means the animal Americans call a moose (a łoś). The caribou is the North American name for the reindeer.

Canada

  • Canada has 48 national parks and national park reserves.
  • Wood Buffalo (Alberta and the Northwest Territories) is the largest.
  • Banff (1885) is the oldest and the most popular.
  • Kluane (Yukon) has Mount Logan, Canada's largest icefields and many grizzly bears.
  • The Icefields Parkway, one of the world's great drives (227 km / 141 miles), links Jasper with Lake Louise past the Columbia Icefield and the Athabasca Glacier.
  • Canada has 22 UNESCO World Heritage Sites, among them the Rocky Mountain parks, Old Quebec, Dinosaur Provincial Park in Alberta (35 kinds of dinosaurs lived there) and Writing-on-Stone / Áísínai'pi, where the Blackfoot people left rock art.

  • the highest tides: the Bay of Fundy, up to 16 m

  • the fourth largest river system in the world: the Mississippi with the Missouri

Where the sources disagree, or slip

A test question may copy its source, so know both versions.

  • The Missouri's length: the USA Facts page gives 3,767 km / 2,341 miles; Ducksters gives 2,540 miles. Both say it is the longest river.
  • Denali's height: 6,190 m / 20,310 ft on the USA Facts page; Ducksters still has the older 20,237 ft and the name Mount McKinley. In 2025 the US government brought back the name Mount McKinley; Alaska and the national park still say Denali. In 2025 it also renamed the Gulf of Mexico the Gulf of America. The sources use Denali and the Gulf of Mexico.
  • Death Valley: National Geographic's heading puts it in Nevada. Most of the park is in California, with a part in Nevada.
  • National Geographic's heading says "Grand Sand Dunes"; the park is called Great Sand Dunes.
  • The USA Facts page gives Badwater Basin as "86 m / 282 ft": that is below sea level.
  • Canada Facts' FAQ puts Mount Logan in the Rocky Mountains. It is in the Saint Elias Mountains, in Kluane National Park, as the same page says elsewhere.
  • Canada Facts puts Wood Buffalo in Alberta; it lies in Alberta and the Northwest Territories.
  • Canada's area is 3.85 million sq mi; the FAQ's "3.58 million" swaps two digits.
  • Butter Insurance calls all four eastern provinces "the Maritimes". Strictly, the Maritimes are New Brunswick, Nova Scotia and Prince Edward Island; with Newfoundland and Labrador they are the Atlantic provinces. It also calls Manitoba and Saskatchewan "the Prairies"; usually Alberta is the third Prairie province.
  • Canadian Sky spells the waterfall "Takkakaw"; the name is Takakkaw Falls.
  • The moose: up to 1.8 m and 725 kg (WWF), or a bull up to 2.1 m and 700 kg (Canada Facts).
  • The USA Facts page gives Washington, D.C. "about 7.5 million inhabitants". The city itself has far fewer (about 700,000)*.
  • WWF calls the Canada lynx one of three lynx species; most lists count four, with the bobcat*.
